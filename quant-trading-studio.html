01

The two enemies

Luck is profit from a moment, not a method. Overfitting is a strategy quietly shaped to fit the past it was tested on. Both draw beautiful equity curves.

Act 1 · Structure pillar

How much of this year came from three trades?

A backtest reports a healthy profit factor and a clean-looking curve. The Structure pillar asks something ruder: delete the three biggest winners and recompute. If what remains can no longer pay for the losses, the edge was three trades wearing a strategy's clothes.

scripts/backtest/analytics.py:1057

def _edge_fragility(profits):
    """Remove top N winning trades and recalculate PF.
    If PF collapses -> strategy depends on outlier wins,
    not consistent noise capture."""
    sorted_desc  = sorted(profits, reverse=True)
    total_profit = sum(p for p in profits if p > 0)   # gross, not net
    top3_profit  = sum(sorted_desc[:3])
    pct_top3     = top3_profit / total_profit * 100.0

scripts/backtest/investor_summary.py:224 — the Structure pillar and its cap

_score_higher(win_rate - breakeven_win_rate, 0.0, 0.08) * 28
+ _score_higher(payoff_ratio,           1.0, 2.2)       * 24
+ _score_lower(top3_profit_share_pct,  65.0, 30.0)      * 24
+ _score_higher(pf_without_top3,        1.0, 1.35)      * 24

if win_rate <= be_wr or payoff <= 1.0:  score = min(score, 54)
if 0 < pf_without_top3 < 1.0:           score = min(score, 54)

The second cap is the interesting one: a profit factor below 1.0 after the top three trades are removed means the strategy was profitable only because of three trades. The score is held at 54 no matter how good the rest of the pillar looks. Note also that pct_top3 divides by gross profit, so a strategy with many small wins and heavy losses reads deceptively low here — the pf_without_top3 term is the one that catches it.

02

Making time honest

Journey stages 2 and 3. Before you can test anything you need two things: a number that means the same thing in 2019 and 2026, and a way to cut the timeline that doesn't cheat.

Act 2 · Rolling normalization

Which half of this chart is "high volatility"?

Prices grow. A mean and standard deviation taken over the whole history describe today's large numbers, not the past's — so early history gets painted permanently calm and every signal fires in one era. The statistic has to roll. Tool 1 uses a 252-day rolling percentile rank, not a z-score: percentile is bounded and scale-free, so one fixed threshold works on gold and on BTC alike. Drag the window down to nothing and watch it become the broken version.

scripts/market_analysis/common/stats.py:58 — the tie convention

(less + 0.5 * equal) / len(values)

Mid-rank, not max-rank. A dead flat window returns 0.5 — "neutral" — instead of 1.0. Without it, a market with no movement at all would be labelled record-high volatility.

Volatility is normalized twice: realized vol is a trailing 20-day standard deviation annualised by √252, then vol-of-vol is a 60-day standard deviation of that series, and both are percentile-ranked over 252 days before being blended 0.75 / 0.25. The second layer separates "volatility is high" from "volatility is unstable" — different regimes.

scripts/market_analysis/tool1_labeler/feature_extractor.py:159 — the warmup gate

if len(window) < window_size:
    return []          # never a partial window

Most code would compute a 12-day standard deviation and call it a 20-day one. This returns nothing, and the resulting None propagates all the way to a forced R6 label at confidence 0.10 — the system says "I don't know" rather than guessing.

Act 3 · Causal smoothing

You can't split a price series at random. So where do you cut it?

Neighbouring days move together, so a random train/test split leaks the future into the past. The alternative is to cut by behaviour: label every week by memory × volatility into six regimes, then build in one occurrence and validate on a different occurrence of the same regime. But a label that flips every week is worthless. So the labeler is causal and stubborn — a new regime must survive four consecutive weeks before it may replace the old one. Drag that number and watch the map shred, then go blind.

How it's actually computed

scripts/market_analysis/tool1_labeler/smoother.py:66

def _candidate_is_confirmed(candidate_streak, candidate_confidences, config):
    if candidate_streak >= config.confirm_weeks:          # 4
        return True
    return (
        candidate_streak >= config.high_conf_confirm_weeks   # 3
        and len(candidate_confidences) >= config.high_conf_confirm_weeks
        and all(c >= config.high_confidence_threshold        # 0.75
                for c in candidate_confidences)
    )

Two doors, not one. Four ordinary weeks, or three consecutive weeks that were each confident above 0.75. While a candidate is pending, the week keeps the old label, is flagged transition_flag=True, and loses 0.15 of its confidence. Transition weeks are then given a training weight of exactly 0.0 — they are never used as learning targets.

scripts/market_analysis/tool1_labeler/validator.py:233 — the map grades itself

FAIL if median segment duration < 2 weeks
FAIL if >= 35% of segments are 1 week long
FAIL if regime changes per year > 12
WARN at < 4 weeks / >= 20% / > 8

The still-running final segment is dropped before grading (service.py:246) — the current, unfinished regime never counts toward validation. It is the most interesting segment on the chart and it is deliberately excluded, because its duration is unknowable until it ends.

The volatility slider matters more than it looks: high_vol_threshold is a single float that decides R1↔R2, R3↔R4 and R5↔R6 simultaneously. The app ships exactly three presets — 0.48, 0.55, 0.62 — and the default sits above 0.50, biased toward calling things calm.

03

One idea, thousands of configs

Journey stage 4. A single alpha isn't a strategy — it's a parameter space. Picking the highest peak in that space is the textbook definition of overfitting, so the pipeline is built to refuse.

Act 4 · Coverage

How do you know the search actually explored anything?

The obvious check is coverage: did the search visit every region of every parameter? With 16,000 genetic-algorithm rows the answer is always yes — every value gets visited eventually, coverage reads 100%, and the gap-finder never fires once. So coverage was redefined to ask a harder question: did the search find a near-best score in every region? Drag the bar from the broken metric to the real one.

How it's actually computed

scripts/optimizer/auto/coverage.py:16 — the docstring is the argument

"""Coverage now requires QUALITY, not just visitation.

A bin is "covered" only if its best score is at least
``quality_ratio * global_best_score``. With raw GA output any param
value gets visited eventually (16k+ rows), so visit-based coverage
is always 1.0 and Step 2 never fires. Quality-based coverage instead
asks "did GA find a near-best score for every region?"."""

scripts/optimizer/auto/coverage.py:34

quality_ratio     = max(0.0, min(0.999, float(quality_ratio)))   # default 0.7
quality_threshold = best_score * quality_ratio if best_score > 0 else -inf
covered_mask      = point_best_scores >= np.float32(quality_threshold)

Gaps are then maximal runs of consecutive uncovered bins, filtered by min_gap_ratio = 0.05, and ranked by gap_ratio × 0.5 + distance_to_best × 0.5. The second term exists to fix a real bug: ranking by gap size alone always pointed the next search back at the widest band next to the current best, which is exactly the basin it had already explored. Half the weight goes to "how far is this from where we already are".

Honest note. This ranking no longer drives a search. The Analyze phase used to feed its gaps into a second genetic run; that path was removed because the diversity-aware seed loop already explored the joint space at least as well (pipeline.py:484). build_unified_challenge_set is still imported at pipeline.py:16 and never called, and the exploration_mode dropdown in the UI now changes no output at all. Coverage is diagnostics — real, computed on every run, written to coverage_summary.json, and read by a human rather than a machine.
Act 5 · Filter

Out of five thousand configurations, which do you keep?

Keeping the top scorers guarantees near-clones of one lucky peak. So the filter clusters every row — nothing truncated — and keeps one representative per cluster. The detail that carries the whole idea: the feature matrix is built from parameters only. Score is never a feature. Toggle it on and watch every "diverse" survivor collapse into the same basin.

dataset.score_vector exists, is loaded, and is never appended to blocks. Clusters are shapes in parameter space. Score enters exactly once, later, when choosing which member of a cluster to keep.

k is auto-tuned by silhouette over a grid that widens with search depth — quick (5,10,15,20), standard (…30,40), thorough (…60,80) — with k-means++ init and a fixed seed of 0, so the same dataset always yields the same representatives.

Honest note. My journal claims I picked the config with the best average return/risk. Half true. The set of survivors is genuinely chosen without reference to score, which is the real anti-overfitting mechanism. But the pick inside each cluster is still a maximum on one metric (representatives.py:384): conservative takes max Sharpe, aggressive takes max Profit, and the default balance takes max of the optimization criterion — which, at the default setting, resolves to Profit. No preset optimizes return/risk. "I stopped selecting by max profit alone" is defensible. "I select by return/risk" is not what the code does.
Act 6 · Train / Validate

Three out-of-sample windows disagree. Now what?

The intuitive answer — and the one my own README still describes — is to take the worst window: minimum profit factor, maximum drawdown, fail on any sign flip. That is not what runs. Each window carries a role weight, metrics combine as a role-weighted mean, and candidates are ranked by Stability = 100·(1 − CV): consistency, not severity. Push one window into a loss and compare the two readings.

ui/Optimization/Stage0TrainValidateModel.cs:130

double mean    = WeightedMean(samples);
double sd      = WeightedStdev(samples);
double scale   = Math.Max(Math.Abs(mean), Math.Max(meanFloor, 1e-9));
double cv      = sd / scale;
return 100d * (1d - Math.Min(cv, 1d));      // Stability

Mean floors — PF 0.5, Sharpe 0.5, Recovery 0.5, Profit 100.0 — stop a near-zero mean from producing an infinite coefficient of variation. Fewer than two contributing windows returns 0 rather than a flattering number: no sample, no score.

04

Capital, and where it broke

Journey stages 5 and 6. An edge is not automatically worth money. And the number I trusted most turned out to be the one I should have read first.

Act 7 · The five pillars

Can a strategy score 100 on four pillars and still be rejected?

Edge, Risk, Structure, Timing, Investment. Investment carries 18% of the weighted score, which makes it look like the least important of the five. It isn't. If Investment returns NOT WORTH CAPITAL, Overall is capped at 39 and the verdict is forced to REJECT no matter what the other four say. Max out the first four, then drag Investment down: the number falls off a cliff, not a slope.

Investment's own score is what trips the veto, and its cliff edges are explicit: month_rr < 0.45 caps it at 49, < 0.65 caps it at 66. That 0.65 is the "fair allocation" line. Under 8 weekly observations the pillar caps itself at 55 regardless of how good it looks — a sample-size honesty cap.

The "risk" in that ratio is not a standard deviation. expected_month_risk is the mean of the worst 35% of losing months — a conditional tail expectation. It is the closest thing in the repository to the return/risk my journal talks about, it is computed per config in the Backtest report, and it is never used to select anything.

Act 8 · Portfolio construction

How many lots of each?

With a handful of proven strategies and a dollar drawdown budget, the manager enumerates every feasible integer-lot combination. Generation prunes with an additive bound — the sum of each strategy's own drawdown — which assumes zero diversification. Admission to the frontier then prices each survivor on the actual merged daily path, which captures correlation exactly. Two contradictory assumptions about the same portfolio, four hundred lines apart.

next_risk = risk_used + lot_risk        # lot_risk = risk_per_lot * lot

scripts/portfolio/service.py:2102 — pricing captures correlation exactly

merged_daily_array = daily_matrix @ scale_vector    # (days × EAs) @ (lots per EA)

There is no covariance matrix anywhere in this pipeline and no sqrt(wᵀΣw). Every candidate portfolio is one matrix-vector product against a pre-built time-aligned daily matrix, so historical correlation is captured implicitly and perfectly — twenty thousand portfolios priced by a single BLAS loop. It is genuinely better than a covariance approximation. It is also not Markowitz, and the frontier is a Pareto staircase over (monthly σ, monthly mean), not a smooth hull.

Monthly risk is statistics.pstdev of calendar-month totals, and the estimation window is the intersection of every strategy's backtest (common_start = max(starts)). Add one alpha with nine months of history to six with five years, and the entire risk model is estimated on nine monthly observations.

Act 9 · The constant

Why was the real drawdown 30% worse than the model?

I wrote in my journal that the market surprised me — that the portfolio was stable but the drawdown came in about thirty percent worse than modelled, and that I ran out of capital before I could tell skill from luck. Reading my own source to build this page, I found the line that gates every portfolio admitted to the frontier.

scripts/portfolio/service.py:899 
historical_dd = max_drawdown_dollar(0.0, daily_values) if daily_values else 0.0
recent_dd     = float(edge_metrics.get("recent_max_dd_dollar", 0.0) or 0.0)
expected_max_dd = max(
    historical_dd * 0.75,
    recent_dd * 1.15,
    expected_tail_loss * 2.0,
)

When the first term wins — which it does for any portfolio whose worst stretch was one sustained event rather than a recent one — a portfolio is admitted to the frontier when 0.75 × its own already-observed drawdown fits inside the budget. Which means the drawdown it had already had, in the backtest, on my screen, could be 1 ÷ 0.75 = 1.333× the budget I set. Drag the multiplier back to 1.00 and watch the gap close.

The honest number was right there

The true peak-to-trough drawdown of the merged portfolio path is computed — at service.py:2113 — stored on the row as max_dd_dollar, and emitted in the payload the UI reads. It is simply never the value compared against the budget. Only expected_max_dd_dollar gates the frontier. The truth was calculated, carried, displayed, and not used.

And the drawdown it measures isn't the drawdown you feel

Every drawdown in the system — per-strategy, per-portfolio, expected, recent — traces back to one map built at service.py:481, which sums closed-trade profit into daily buckets. An open position's unrealized loss contributes exactly zero until the day it closes. A −$500 morning that recovers to −$50 by the close is recorded as −$50.

Margin and stop-outs are driven by equity. This model never sees equity. Stack that on top of the 0.75 and the two errors point the same direction.

The instruments existed. They were never wired in.

scripts/portfolio/metrics.py contains working implementations of exactly the measurements that would have caught this. A repository-wide search for callers returns the function definitions and nothing else.

co_drawdown_jaccard measures the fraction of time two strategies are underwater simultaneously. tail_dependence measures how much more often they crash together than independence would predict. bootstrap_drawdown_p95 resamples blocks of the equity path — preserving autocorrelation — to ask how bad the drawdown gets in the 5% of futures that go worst. The two failure modes I actually hit had a detector each, written, tested against nothing, and left unreferenced.

Coda

What "clean" actually means

As a startup this failed. The final portfolio really was stable; the alpha I trusted really was steady, and never produced a breakout; the one I ranked second quietly made the money. I ran out of capital and time before I could prove whether any of it was skill.

  1. A clean model isn't the one that makes the most money. It's the one you can prove is right in the shortest time.
  2. My mistake wasn't the math. It was never budgeting the time-to-proof.
  3. Clean models look simple not because nobody could make them complex, but because simple is what clean looks like.
  4. And a model you can't audit isn't clean, however simple it looks. The 0.75 sat on line 902 for the entire life of this project. It cost me a third of a risk budget I thought I had set. I did not find it by losing money — losing money only told me something was wrong. I found it by reading my own source, line by line, to write this page.
